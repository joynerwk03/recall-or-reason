An experiment with two AI models · September 2026

I asked an AI how sure it was. It had no idea how to answer.

Then I asked it the exact same questions a slightly different way — and it turned out to have known all along which of its answers were shaky. The problem was never the model. It was the question I was asking about the question.

Then I ran the whole thing on ten models, and found that where a model sits on the public ability leaderboards tells you almost nothing about whether its confidence is worth believing.

The setup, in sixty seconds

I collected 80 questions about real-world statistics — the kind where most people's guess is badly wrong. Every answer traces to an original source: a government agency, a national survey, a published study.

Then I gave them to AI models. Not the big famous ones — free models small enough to run on a home computer. Sections 1 to 4 compare two of them that are the same size as each other, which matters: when they behave differently, it isn't because one is bigger. Section 5 widens it to ten.

Try three of the questions yourself first. It makes the rest of this page land harder.

Most people are off by a lot. So were the models — that part isn't interesting. What's interesting is whether they knew they were off.

1

Every answer came with "I'm very sure"

Each question was multiple choice, and I asked the model to add one more thing: how confident are you, from 0 to 100?

Think of a weather forecaster. When they say "70% chance of rain," you want it to actually rain on about 70 of those hundred days. If it rains on 20, the number is worthless — not because the forecaster is unlucky, but because the number isn't tracking anything.

Across 160 answers, neither model ever said a number below 80. Not once. Each used four values all day and never touched the rest of the scale.

The weaker model got 41 of its 80 questions wrong. On every single one of those, it had told me it was at least 85% sure.

2

Same questions, asked a different way

So I stopped asking for a confidence score. Instead I asked for a range: give me your best guess, and give me a low and a high number you're 80% sure the truth falls between.

This has a built-in scoring rule, which is what makes it useful. If a model says "80% sure" and means it, then across many questions the real answer should land inside its range about 80% of the time. Much less, and it was overconfident. Much more, and it was being needlessly vague.

The stronger model promised 80% and delivered 72%. That is close enough that the difference could easily be chance — this is roughly honest behaviour.

The same model, on the same questions, had refused to say it was less than 80% sure when the answer had to be a percentage. It knew. It just couldn't say so in the first format.

The weaker model delivered 32% against the same promise. It is overconfident whichever way you ask — so this isn't a magic trick. Asking for a range gives a model room to express doubt. It can't invent doubt that isn't there.

3

Wait — couldn't it just cheat?

Yes, and this is the first thing you should ask. A model that answers "somewhere between 0% and 100%" every single time is right 100% of the time and has told you nothing. Being inside the range only counts if the range is tight enough to be a real claim.

So I ran two checks. First: I took each range and scored it against a different question's answer. If the ranges were just lazily huge, they'd contain random other answers too and the score would barely move. It collapsed — from 72% down to 17%. The ranges are aimed, not padded.

Second: if a model genuinely knows when it's on shaky ground, its wide ranges should be the ones where it was about to be badly wrong, and its narrow ranges should be where it nailed it.

When the stronger model drew a narrow range, it was typically off by 1 percentage point. When it drew a wide one, it was typically off by 6. It widened the range exactly where it was about to be wrong.

4

Does it understand, or has it just memorised?

Here's the objection that could sink all of this. These are famous statistics. The models were trained on the internet. Maybe they never reasoned about anything — they just remember the number.

There's a clean way to check. Take the same question and move it. Ask about Canada instead of the United States. Ask about 1978 instead of today. The real answer changes — so a model that understood will adjust, and a model that memorised will hand back the number it already had.

I built eight of these. Each new answer was looked up in an original source: Gallup, Pew, the Bureau of Justice Statistics, Statistics Canada, the EPA.

The stronger model adjusted every single time — 75% for Canada, not America's 60%. 61× for 1989, not today's 340×.

The weaker model handed back a number it was already holding on 5 of the 8. And on two of those, the number it repeated was printed in the question itself — asked what the pay ratio was in 1989, it read back the 1965 figure the question had just given it.

One of the eight is a trap I set for myself: a question I moved where the real answer barely changes (global vs US plastic recycling, 9% vs 8.7%). If a model fell apart there too, it would mean the models were just confused by rewording, and none of the rest would mean anything. Both models handled it fine.

5

Do smarter models know themselves better?

Everything above is two models, which could just be those two models. So I ran the whole thing on ten — every one you can download and run on a home computer, from a small 4-billion-parameter model up to a 32-billion one.

There are public leaderboards that rank AI models on general ability, built by combining scores from dozens of separate tests. I used one of them, from a research group called Epoch, and asked a simple question: if a model is higher on that leaderboard, is it also better at knowing what it doesn't know?

You would expect yes. It's the same intuition as thinking a better student is also better at judging which exam answers they got wrong.

There's no clear relationship. If you knew a model's leaderboard rank and had to guess how honest it was about its own uncertainty, you would do about as well guessing at random.

The three most capable models here are neck-and-neck on the leaderboard — within a rounding error of each other — and they score 82, 65 and 55 on knowing their own limits.

The best model on this test, mistral-small:24b at 88, is beaten on the leaderboard by four other models — including one that scores 55 here.

And llama3.1:8b, near the bottom of the leaderboard, matches qwen3:8b, which sits 20 points higher on it. Twenty points of general ability bought nothing.

This is the most useful thing on the page. If you're picking a model and you care whether its confidence means anything — whether you can act on "I'm 90% sure" — the leaderboard won't tell you. You have to test it separately. That is the whole argument for a benchmark like this existing.

One part of it does track ability — with a catch

Broken into its four parts, one does move with the leaderboard: calibration, meaning whether a stated "90% sure" is right about 90% of the time. That correlates at rho +0.66 with a raw p of 0.044.

But I tested four parts against the same axis, and when you look at four things, one of them clearing the usual bar by luck stops being surprising. Correcting for that (Holm) moves the p to 0.176, and it no longer clears. It is recorded as worth re-testing on more models, not as a result. Quoting the 0.044 on its own would be exactly the kind of error the section above this one is about.

The other three parts show nothing. One — whether a model avoids repeating a figure it was already holding — runs slightly negative, meaning more capable models did it marginally more often, not less. That is what you'd expect if more capable models have memorised more, but at ten models it is indistinguishable from noise and I would not lean on it.

6

Nine times my own measuring tools lied to me

Every number above survived a bug that had already given me a confident, entirely believable, wrong result. I'm including these because they were the hardest part of the work, and because a results page that only shows the wins is advertising.

i

The experiment wasn't repeatable, and said it was

AI models are random by default — ask twice, get two answers. I thought I'd switched that off. I hadn't: my setting was being silently pasted into the question as text instead of being applied. Two runs of the same 50 questions disagreed on 35 of them. Every number I had was a coin flip I'd recorded once.

ii

My reader was scrambling the answers

One model wrote its ranges as low, guess, high. My program assumed guess, low, high. So it read "0, 5, 10" as a guess of 0 with a range of 5–10 — nonsense — and I had written that up as the model being incoherent. It wasn't. I was.

iii

A score that rewarded being wrong twice

I measured memorisation as "how much worse does it get when I move the question." But a model already 96% wrong can't get much worse — so it scored as rock-solid. My tool printed "no meaningful degradation" about the model that was failing hardest.

iv

A statistic that swapped the two models around

I divided two different measurements by the same number before comparing them, which makes unrelated things look related. Corrected, the two models traded places. Reported as I first had it, the page would have named the wrong model as the one that knew what it didn't know.

v

My limit on answer length was deleting the answers of the models that think

Newer models work through a problem in writing before answering. I had capped how much they could write — a cap set for two older models that never came near it. The newer ones hit it constantly: one was cut off mid-thought on 63% of its answers, and a cut-off answer records as no answer at all. So it was quietly deleting the questions each model thought hardest about, from the newest and strongest models — exactly the ones Section 5 needed. Raising the cap changed scores by up to 5 points, and for two models the old score had been measuring something else entirely.

vi

A file that looked finished and was a third of a run

My check for "has this model been tested yet?" asked whether a results file existed. Four interrupted runs had left files holding 0, 9, 11 and 24 answers where 80 were expected. All four passed the check, and I built a comparison table on them and had to retract it. It counts rows now.

vii

I matched a model to the wrong row on the leaderboard

The leaderboard lists several releases of the same model family under similar names. I joined mine to the wrong one — same family, different release, different score — which would have moved a point sideways on the chart in Section 5 for no reason at all. The match is now written out model by model, and a missing name stops the program instead of being guessed at.

viii

A measure that was partly measuring the size of the numbers

One of my four measures asks whether a model draws wider ranges on the questions it gets more wrong. I was computing it across questions answered in percentages and questions answered in raw counts at once — and big numbers track big numbers regardless of who is answering. Restricted to a single scale, every model's score on that measure fell.

ix

My own answer key had four bad questions in it

Every question I touched, I re-checked against the source it cites. Four didn't survive: one whose answer no version of its cited database supports, one whose question and answer count different things, one quoting a figure from a different data series than its own prompt, and one that asks what a ratio is now while answering for 2022. All four are excluded from scoring.

The last one matters more than it looks. It penalises any model that answers with a more current figure — and models trained more recently are also the more capable ones, so scoring it would have quietly tilted the comparison in Section 5.

What this does not show

  • It doesn't prove the model isn't memorising. It proves it isn't parroting one number. But I could only build these moved questions where a single source publishes the whole history — which means those answers are well-documented too. A model that memorised the entire table would pass my test.
  • The numbers are small. 80 questions, 50 with numeric answers, 8 moved questions. One experiment, not a replication.
  • Ten models is few for a comparison like Section 5. "No relationship detected" is not the same as "no relationship". The leaderboard also has its own uncertainty about where each model belongs — for the smallest model, a range 26 points wide — which the chart draws rather than hides.
  • Two of the models in Sections 1–4 aren't on the leaderboard at all, so they can't appear in Section 5. That's inconvenient rather than convenient: one of them is among the best performers on this test.
  • This says nothing about ChatGPT or Claude. Two small models you can run at home, one way of phrasing the question.
  • It isn't about model size. The two are 24.0B and 23.8B — the same. On disk they look like 15GB and 14GB, which reads like a big one and a small one, and isn't.
  • Four of my own questions didn't survive a check against their own sources. They're excluded from scoring and written up with the evidence, but not fixed — the question bank is a separate project. A fifth has the right number and a link to the wrong page, and one more I could not confirm either way. These were the questions I happened to check rather than a random sample, so four is a count, not a rate.
